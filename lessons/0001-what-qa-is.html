What QA Is — and Why You're Already Halfway There

Your healthcare skills translated into the language of software quality.

⏱ 20 minutes 🎯 1 concept, 5 vocabulary terms ✍️ 1 exercise

1. The One-Sentence Definition of QA

Quality Assurance (QA) is the discipline of making sure software does what it's supposed to do — before it reaches real users.

That's it. Everything else — test cases, bug reports, JIRA, Agile sprints — is just the toolkit used to make that happen.

Healthcare Parallel

Before a patient is discharged from Royal Surrey, a checklist is followed: medications reviewed, mobility assessed, follow-up booked, patient safety confirmed. Nobody gets discharged until the checks pass.

QA is exactly this — a structured, documented process that software goes through before it's "discharged" to users. You have been doing QA-style thinking every day at work.

2. Three Terms Everyone Confuses

In almost every Junior QA interview, you will be asked: "What's the difference between QA, QC, and Testing?" Get this right and you immediately stand out.

Term What it means Your healthcare equivalent
QA
Quality Assurance		 Proactive. Sets up the processes and standards so that defects don't occur in the first place. Infection control protocols on the ward. You don't wait for an outbreak — you prevent it with standards everyone follows.
QC
Quality Control		 Reactive. Checks the output of a process against a standard to find defects. Checking a medication dose against the prescription before giving it to a patient.
Testing A specific activity within QC. Actually running the software to find defects. Physically pressing the call bell to verify it rings at the nurses' station.
The Key Insight

QA is proactive (prevents defects). QC is reactive (detects defects). Testing is a subset of QC — it's one specific technique. Most Junior QA roles involve both QA activities (reviewing requirements) and testing activities (executing test cases). The job title says "QA" but the work is really both.

Your Interview Answer

"QA is process-focused and preventive — it builds standards so defects don't occur. QC is product-focused and detective — it checks outputs against expectations. Testing is a specific activity within QC where you actually execute the software to find defects. In a Junior QA role, I'd be involved in all three: reviewing requirements early to prevent misunderstandings, checking outputs against acceptance criteria, and executing test cases to find bugs."

3. Verification vs Validation

Two more terms that often come up:

TermQuestion it answersExample
Verification "Are we building the product right?" — against the spec Checking that the login page was built exactly as the design document specified
Validation "Are we building the right product?" — against user needs Giving the login page to real users and checking they can actually log in without confusion
Healthcare Parallel

Verification = "Did I follow the care protocol correctly?" (checking against the written standard)

Validation = "Did following the protocol actually help the patient?" (checking against the real-world outcome)

4. The Five Vocabulary Words for This Lesson

Learn these cold. You'll use them in every conversation from here on.

TermDefinitionHealthcare analogy
Defect / Bug When software behaves differently from how it should A medication dispensing machine giving the wrong dose — a deviation from expected behaviour
Test Case A documented set of steps to verify one specific piece of behaviour A clinical procedure checklist — steps defined in advance, expected outcome known
Expected Result What SHOULD happen when you run the test Patient's blood pressure should be within normal range after medication
Actual Result What ACTUALLY happened when you ran the test Patient's blood pressure reading after medication was administered
Pass / Fail Actual result matched expected (Pass) or didn't (Fail) Vitals within range = Pass. Out of range = Fail → escalate

5. Why Your Background Is an Asset, Not a Gap

Many career switchers apologise for their background in interviews. Don't. Here is what your healthcare experience actually demonstrates to a hiring manager:

  • Protocol discipline — You know how to follow a procedure precisely, in order, without skipping steps. This is exactly what executing a test case requires.
  • Documentation accuracy — Patient records must be factual, reproducible, and clear. Bug reports are the same.
  • Outcome verification — You already think in terms of "expected vs actual." That's the core of every test case.
  • Escalation judgement — Knowing when to raise a concern vs handle it yourself. QAs must decide when a bug is critical vs low priority.
  • High-stakes mindset — You work in an environment where mistakes hurt real people. That care and rigour is exactly what healthcare IT, fintech, and regulated industries are hiring for.
Your Interview Narrative

"My healthcare background gives me a natural QA mindset — I'm trained to follow protocols precisely, document observations accurately, compare actual outcomes against expected ones, and escalate when something is wrong. In healthcare, errors affect patients. That same rigour applies to software, especially in regulated industries like healthcare IT. I'm not starting from zero — I'm translating skills I already use every day."
